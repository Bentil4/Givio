I.

Four Voices in the Room

Role Playing put four people in four chairs — the Organizer, the Operator, the Admin, the Family — and asked each what they want and what they're afraid of. The coach then leaned on two of them to see where the guarantees would actually have to hold.

Stakeholder

The Organizer

Wants

  • +Each event managed independently.
  • +The platform handling many events at once, without degrading.

Fears

  • −Events mixing together — no separation between them.
  • −The application buckling under concurrent organizers and events.
Coach raises the stakes Picture Organizer B — a rival company on the same platform. They don't just want their data unmixed. They want to never even discover Organizer A exists: no shared IDs, no leaked names in a URL or an error message, no operator addable across tenants by mistake.

Stakeholder

The Operator

Wants

  • +To see only the events they're assigned to — never the Organizer's other events.
  • +To manage only what they're assigned to.
  • +When on several events at once, to see all of them and choose which to work first.
Coach raises the stakes An event-switcher — tabs, a workspace picker — so "which event am I acting in right now" is unmistakable on screen at all times. A scoped check-in action should never be able to fire against the wrong event.

Stakeholder

The Admin

Wants

  • +To manage many Organizer accounts and events independently, one never affecting another.
  • +Organizers to sign up, then wait for Admin's review and confirmation before getting in.
  • +Before approving: company name, location, size, estimated users, company type, and other onboarding facts.

Fears

  • −Organizers seeing or managing other Organizers' accounts or events.
  • −Organizers holding full admin privileges.
  • −Admin itself losing full system control.

Stakeholder

The Family

Wants

  • +Live, accurate donation totals when viewing through the family code.
  • +On entry, to be asked whether this is their own phone — and if not, to be logged out automatically the moment they leave.

Fears

  • −The wrong family member — or a stranger — gaining access to donation details.
  • −Not seeing every donation actually entered — a partial or hidden total.
II.

Bedrock, and What Sits On It

First Principles Thinking tested eight candidate facts about how Givio actually works. Five held their weight. Three didn't — true, but not load-bearing for a role system — and were set aside.

1

Multiple unrelated event companies share the platform concurrently.

2

One event company runs several events concurrently.

3

Recorder and viewer are different people, in different places.

4

Events are temporary and bounded.

5

The family didn't choose the platform — the Organizer brought them onto it.

6

Access to an event must be auditable at any moment.

7

Connectivity at the event is unreliable.

8

One person could work across more than one event company over time.

Everything else in the session was built on the five that stayed:

An Operator logs in with credentials the Organizer sent them — scoped to that Organizer — rather than one identity that switches between events or companies.

Two paths onto the platform for an Organizer: Admin creates and invites them directly, or they self-sign-up and set their own credentials, but stay pending until Admin approves.

Confirmed as bedrock: access to event or money data is always granted per event, never at the company level — even an Organizer's access to their own events is an implicit per-event grant, not a blanket company permission.

One human always equals one Organizer account. Two people at the same company need two separate accounts, never shared access under one identity.

The first Organizer at a company is a Super Organizer — able to add other people as Organizer too, by the same mechanism used to add an Operator, so every person keeps their own identity for audit and security.

Hierarchy rule: an added Organizer can add and manage Operators, but cannot add another Organizer. Only the Super Organizer can add co-Organizers, and only the Super Organizer can manage or revoke either co-Organizers or Operators.

Revoking access never erases attribution: a person's name stays permanently attached to every donation they entered, even after they can no longer log in. Revocation removes access — not history. Decision · First Principles

A family's read-only live view survives even if Admin suspends the entire Organizer account. Suspension blocks the Organizer and Operator's write and login access — not the family's view, which may freeze or may keep updating. Decision · First Principles

III.

Two Roads Down to the Root

Five Whys was run twice — once on the approval gate that stands between an Organizer and the platform, once on why an Operator only ever sees their own events. The first chain has a false floor halfway down. The second is short, and lands somewhere the whole session kept returning to.

Why does Admin approve every Organizer account?

Why #1
To manage organizer access to the platform, for future billing purposes.
Why #2
Nothing would actually go wrong if Organizers self-activated instantly — billing alone does not require pre-approval. False floor · billing isn't the real reason
Why #3
Without approval, the platform becomes a vector for scams — unvetted organizers could defraud grieving families, and the platform's credibility collapses if it's seen as enabling that.
Why #4
After-the-fact bans don't work: a scammer just abandons the account and opens a new one for the next event, skipping out on the bill and the consequences each time.
Resulting feature Detect potential duplicate events — the same event, or the same deceased, registered more than once to double-collect donations — and notify Admin when it happens.

Why are Operators scoped to only their assigned events?

Why #1
Confidentiality and data protection — a generic privacy policy.
Root
It ultimately protects the grieving family's dignity — being seen, in their grief and their finances, by a stranger who has no relationship to their event.

Not a data-privacy policy. A dignity guarantee. Insight · Five Whys

The six threads carried out of Themes 2–4 were resolved one by one:

✓

Co-Organizer bypass, closed. The same identity checks used at Organizer signup — cross-referenced against banned or rejected applicants — apply whenever a co-Organizer is added, and Admin is notified on every addition. No separate vetting system needed.

✓

Company verification, manual for v1. Admin reviews the submitted info plus a document upload — business registration or ID — and a phone verification call, before approving. Automated third-party verification is deferred.

✓

Family-as-organizer: Won't — this time. V1 stays scoped to the professional event-company Organizer model. Self-service family accounts are parked as a future direction.

✓

Audit log follows the tenant boundary. Admin sees the platform-wide log across every Organizer. A Super Organizer sees only their own company's trail — co-Organizers and Operators — never another company's.

✓

Support, for v1, is a form. A simple, logged "Contact Admin" form — not a full ticketing system, which is deferred as a later Should.

✓

The consolidated total is near-real-time. Same consistency bar as the family's live view, with a periodic settlement and export report as a secondary accounting feature.

V. · Synthesis

Five Things the Whole System Stands On

Everything above — four voices, five bedrock facts, two chains of why, thirteen questions — settles into five decisions the PRD cannot compromise on.

01

The unit of accessThe per-event grant is the sole atomic unit of access control, across every tier — Admin, Organizer, Operator alike.

02

The shape of isolationCredentials-per-relationship makes tenant isolation structural, not policy-enforced — there is no shared identity left to leak across a boundary.

03

What the gate is forThe approval gate exists for fraud and trust protection, not billing — and that's what drives onboarding verification, duplicate-event detection, and the co-Organizer bypass fix.

04

What scoping protectsFamily dignity — not a generic privacy policy — is the real reason for strict Operator scoping, and should frame the PRD's own language.

05

What revocation meansRevocation removes access but never erases attribution. No one who has touched money can ever be hard-deleted.
